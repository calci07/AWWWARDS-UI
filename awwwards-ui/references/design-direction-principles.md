# Design direction principles

Use this reference before route creation and again in Stage B. It adapts the six techniques demonstrated in [6 EASY Tips to 10x Any Site's Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pbhLsV-Dyho) by Self-Made Web Designer (published 2026-03-05). Treat them as design reasoning, not fixed stylistic defaults.

## Quick reference

| Principle | Required design decision | Rejection trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Headline anchor | Let the display/headline voice establish personality; choose supporting text roles from the same world with legible contrast | Fonts are nearly identical, tonally unrelated, unlicensed, inaccessible, or selected only because they are fashionable |
| Narrative star | Give the page one dominant memorable element tied to product truth and the conversion story | The star is generic spectacle, competes with the CTA/proof, or could belong unchanged to any brand |
| Visual rhyming | Derive a small motif vocabulary from the star and repeat it with variation across type, shapes, crops, controls, or motion | Repetition becomes decoration, mixed metaphors, or every component receives the motif |
| Quiet depth | Use material, texture, layering, light, and restrained spatial cues to make the experience tangible | Glass, noise, shadow, 3D, or texture becomes a second star or reduces readability/performance |
| Emphasis hierarchy | Establish primary, secondary, and tertiary emphasis through scale, weight, color, spacing, and—when contrast remains sufficient—opacity | Fixed opacity percentages replace contrast testing or supporting text becomes faint |
| Divergent exploration | Compare materially different compositions before locking a high-impact star or hero | The first acceptable idea is polished repeatedly while composition, light/dark mode, crop, or star never changes |

## 1. Build typography around an anchor

Choose the headline/display role first when it carries the approved route's personality. Then select body, utility, data, and accent roles that feel related but provide visible contrast in width, construction, rhythm, or historical voice. A superfamily is valid; a cross-foundry pairing is valid; one family is valid. The route, licenses, language coverage, body readability, product UI, and performance decide.

Test pairings in real headline, paragraph, navigation, label, numeral, error, and localized copy. Similar-but-not-matching faces often look accidental. Dramatically different faces fail when they imply different brands. The headline may command attention, but body and controls remain effortless to read.

## 2. Choose one narrative star

Define the star as the primary memorable visual or interaction that answers:

> What one image, object, typographic behavior, data form, or interaction makes this product's truth felt immediately?

Derive it from the product's story. A scheduling product might abstract time slots; a material studio might use tactility; a security product might resolve disorder into a legible signal. Do not select a star only because it looks impressive.

Record the star's narrative job, primary viewport, relationship to the headline/CTA, quiet fallback, mobile form, reduced-motion form, performance budget, and failure risk. Keep one dominant star per viewport. Supporting assets and effects stay subordinate.

## 3. Create visual rhyming

Extract two to four reusable cues from the star: silhouette, crop angle, line behavior, color transition, texture, material, directional motion, or framing device. Reuse those cues selectively so sections feel from the same universe.

Rhyming means transformed recurrence, not copy-paste repetition. A triangular image crop may echo as an arrow terminal or a reveal direction; it should not force every card into a triangle. Functional controls preserve recognizable affordances and accessibility.

## 4. Add quiet depth

Use depth to bridge screen and physical experience: restrained texture, believable lighting, foreground/background overlap, material surfaces, soft contact, or a purposeful 3D element. Depth supports tangibility and hierarchy; it does not compensate for a weak star.

Keep dense copy and forms on quiet fields. Reject arbitrary glass cards, noise, bloom, floating layers, or shadows that compete with content, fracture the visual world, or create expensive motion.

## 5. Grade emphasis, not accessibility

Define primary, secondary, and tertiary emphasis tokens. Use size, weight, spacing, color, and controlled opacity together. Percentages shown in a reference are examples, never universal values.

Every text role still passes the project's contrast target in its actual rendered state, including hover, disabled, dark/light, image-backed, and reduced-motion states. Never lower opacity to make an overcrowded layout feel calmer; fix the hierarchy or content density.

## 6. Explore before polishing

Stage A's exactly three routes must differ in concept, composition, narrative star, and system—not only palette or type. After approval, make low-cost studies for a high-impact star before producing the final master. Compare changes such as layout family, light/dark field, crop, scale, spatial relationship, and star treatment.

Use the smallest study set that reveals a real choice; three to six is normal, while a complex signature asset may justify more. Show contact-sheet reasoning when useful. Select using product relevance, hierarchy, ownability, responsive resilience, accessibility, performance, and production fit. Once locked, build one approved master and derive coordinated assets from it.

## Combined completion check

A direction passes only when the star is product-specific, typography carries a coherent voice, two to four rhyming cues create continuity, depth remains subordinate, emphasis is clear and accessible, and the chosen composition survived meaningful alternatives. These principles strengthen product truth; they never override approval, evidence, accessibility, or responsive behavior.
